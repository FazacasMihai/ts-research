\section{Modeling}\label{sec:model}

This section states the problem, the way missing data is simulated, the
two families of methods that are compared, and the experimental protocol.
The notation follows the code in the project repository, so every formula
below is the one that is actually computed.

\subsection{Problem}

A labelled dataset is a set of pairs $(\mathbf{x}, y)$, where $y \in
\{1, \dots, K\}$ is a class label and $\mathbf{x}$ is a multivariate time
series observed at increasing times,
\[
  \mathbf{x} = \bigl((t_1, x_1), (t_2, x_2), \dots, (t_n, x_n)\bigr),
  \qquad t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^c .
\]
The number of observations $n$ and the gaps $\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$
may differ from one series to another. The task is to learn a classifier
$f$ from a training set such that $f(\mathbf{x})$ predicts $y$ on unseen
series. A model is called \emph{continuous-time} if it uses the
observation times $t_i$ directly instead of assuming one value per fixed
time step.

\subsection{Missing-data model}\label{sec:missing}

All datasets used here are regularly sampled: a series has $T$ points,
at the times $0, 1, \dots, T-1$. Irregular series are obtained by removing
points at random. For a missing fraction $p \in [0, 1)$, each series keeps
\[
  k = \max\bigl(2,\; T - \operatorname{round}(pT)\bigr)
\]
of its points, chosen uniformly without replacement, and every kept point
keeps its original time. Points are removed independently in every
series, so two series of the same dataset have different gaps. The random
generator is seeded, so all methods see exactly the same gaps; the
training and test sets use different seeds. This is data
\emph{missing completely at random}: the chance that a point is removed
does not depend on its value.

\subsection{Mathematical features}\label{sec:features}

The first family turns every series into a fixed-length feature vector
and trains a linear classifier on it. The features are standardised with
the mean and variance of the training set, and the classifier is a
multinomial logistic regression with $L_2$ regularisation, with the
default settings of scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}. No
hyperparameter of this family is tuned.

\subsubsection*{Summary statistics (baseline)}
For every channel: mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum and total
change $x_n - x_1$, which gives $5c$ features. Apart from the first and
the last value, these statistics ignore the order of the points and the
times, and serve as the simplest baseline.

\subsubsection*{Path signatures}
The observations are joined by straight lines into a continuous path
$X \colon [t_1, t_n] \to \mathbb{R}^d$. Time is added as an extra channel,
$X_t = (t, x(t))$, and the path is started at the origin by a basepoint
\cite{morrill2020generalised}, so $d = c + 1$. The signature of $X$ is the
sequence of its iterated integrals \cite{chen1958integration,
lyons2022sigml}: for a word $(i_1, \dots, i_k)$ with
$i_j \in \{1, \dots, d\}$,
\[
  S(X)^{i_1 \dots i_k} =
  \int_{t_1 < u_1 < \dots < u_k < t_n}
  dX^{i_1}_{u_1} \cdots dX^{i_k}_{u_k} .
\]
The signature truncated at depth $m$ keeps all words of length
$1 \le k \le m$, which gives $d + d^2 + \dots + d^m$ numbers. For one
straight segment with increment $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ the level-$k$
term is $\delta^{\otimes k} / k!$, and the signatures of consecutive
segments are combined with Chen's identity
\cite{chen1958integration}, $S(X * Y) = S(X) \otimes S(Y)$, where $*$
joins two paths end to end. The signature therefore works directly with
uneven times: a long gap is simply a long segment of the path. Because
time only increases along the path, no part of it is retraced, and by the
uniqueness theorem of Hambly and Lyons \cite{hambly2010uniqueness} the
full (untruncated) signature determines the path. We use depths $m = 2$
and $m = 3$.

\subsubsection*{Dynamic mode decomposition}
The Koopman operator \cite{koopman1931, mezic2005spectral} describes a
dynamical system through linear dynamics of functions of its state, and
DMD \cite{schmid2010dmd, kutz2016dmd} estimates its leading eigenvalues
from data. DMD needs equally spaced points, so a series with missing
points is first interpolated linearly back onto its original grid of $T$
points. With $q = 5$ delays, the series $s_1, \dots, s_T \in \mathbb{R}^c$
gives the vectors $h_j = (s_j, \dots, s_{j+q-1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{qc}$,
which form the two matrices
$H = [h_1, \dots, h_{N-1}]$ and $H' = [h_2, \dots, h_N]$, with
$N = T - q + 1$. Using the truncated singular value decomposition
$H \approx U_r \Sigma_r V_r^\top$ with $r = 4$, the reduced operator is
\[
  \tilde{A} = U_r^\top H' V_r \Sigma_r^{-1} .
\]
Its eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_r$, ordered by modulus, are the
DMD eigenvalues. Each gives a growth rate $\log\lvert\lambda_k\rvert$ and
a frequency $\lvert\arg\lambda_k\rvert$ per time step, so the method has
$2r = 8$ features.

\subsection{Neural models}\label{sec:neural}

The second family reads the series point by point and updates a hidden
state $h \in \mathbb{R}^H$; a linear layer maps the state after the last
observation to $K$ class scores. All models receive the same input: the
values are normalised per channel with the mean and standard deviation of
the training set, and the times are counted in original time steps, so
$\Delta t_i = 1$ when no point is missing. Series in one batch are padded
to the same length, and padding never changes the output.

\subsubsection*{GRU and LSTM}
The GRU \cite{cho2014gru} and the LSTM \cite{hochreiter1997lstm} are the
standard discrete-time baselines. They do not model time, so the gap is
given to them as an extra input channel: the input at step $i$ is
$(x_i, \Delta t_i)$.

\subsubsection*{CfC}
Closed-form continuous-time networks \cite{hasani2022cfc} replace the
solution of an ODE by a formula in which the elapsed time appears
explicitly. With $u = \phi\bigl(W[x_i; h_{i-1}] + b\bigr)$, where $\phi$
is a scaled $\tanh$, the update is
\begin{align*}
  h_i &= \bigl(1 - \sigma_i\bigr) \odot \tanh(W_f u + b_f)
      + \sigma_i \odot \tanh(W_g u + b_g), \\
  \sigma_i &= \operatorname{sigmoid}\bigl((W_a u + b_a)\,\Delta t_i
      + W_b u + b_b\bigr),
\end{align*}
so the gap $\Delta t_i$ decides how far the state moves from one value
towards the other.

\subsubsection*{LTC}
In liquid time-constant networks \cite{hasani2021ltc} every neuron $k$
follows an ODE whose time constant depends on the input,
\[
  C_k \frac{dv_k}{dt} = g_k\bigl(e_k - v_k\bigr)
  + \sum_j w_{jk}\, \operatorname{sigmoid}\bigl(\gamma_{jk}(v_j - \mu_{jk})\bigr)
    \bigl(E_{jk} - v_k\bigr),
\]
where the sum runs over all neurons $j$ and over the inputs, and the
capacitances $C_k$, conductances $g_k$ and weights $w_{jk}$ are kept
positive by a softplus. Between two observations the ODE is solved with
$6$ semi-implicit Euler steps of length $\delta = \Delta t_i / 6$:
\[
  v_k \leftarrow
  \frac{C_k v_k / \delta + g_k e_k + \sum_j w_{jk} \sigma_{jk} E_{jk}}
       {C_k / \delta + g_k + \sum_j w_{jk} \sigma_{jk}},
  \qquad
  \sigma_{jk} = \operatorname{sigmoid}\bigl(\gamma_{jk}(v_j - \mu_{jk})\bigr).
\]
Because the update divides by $\delta$, the gap of the first point is set
to at least one time step.

\subsubsection*{Neural CDE}
A neural controlled differential equation \cite{kidger2020ncde} lets the
data drive the hidden state. The observations, with time as an extra
channel, are joined by straight lines into a path
$X \colon [t_1, t_n] \to \mathbb{R}^{c+1}$, and
\[
  z(t) = z(t_1) + \int_{t_1}^{t} f_\theta\bigl(z(s)\bigr)\, dX(s),
  \qquad
  z(t_1) = \ell_\theta(t_1, x_1),
\]
where $\ell_\theta$ is a linear map and
$f_\theta(z) = \tanh\bigl(W_2 \operatorname{ReLU}(W_1 z + b_1) + b_2\bigr)$,
reshaped into an $H \times (c+1)$ matrix. On the segment from $t_{i-1}$ to
$t_i$ the path has the constant increment
$\Delta X_i = (t_i, x_i) - (t_{i-1}, x_{i-1})$. The equation is solved
with $n_i = \lceil \Delta t_i \rceil$ steps of the classical fourth-order
Runge--Kutta method, each with increment $\Delta X_i / n_i$, so a long gap
gets as many solver steps as the points it replaces. The class scores are
computed from $z(t_n)$.

\subsubsection*{Training}
All neural models are trained in the same way: cross-entropy loss, the
Adam optimiser, batches of 32 series and at most 500 epochs. Training
stops when the validation loss has not improved for 50 epochs, and the
weights of the best epoch are kept. The hidden size $H \in \{32, 64\}$ and
the learning rate $\eta \in \{10^{-3}, 3 \cdot 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}\}$ are
chosen from the same grid for every model, separately for each dataset,
as the pair with the lowest validation loss on the data without missing
points. The test set is not used for any choice.

\subsubsection*{Protocol}
Every method is run with $p \in \{0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ of the points
removed, using the gaps of Section~\ref{sec:missing}. For $p > 0$ the
experiment is repeated with three seeds, which give three different sets
of gaps; for $p = 0$ there is nothing random to remove, so it is run once.
The feature methods are fitted on the whole training set. The neural
models keep a stratified 20\% of the training set for validation (early
stopping and the choice of $H$ and $\eta$) and are trained on the rest.
The test set is used once, at the end.

\subsubsection*{Baselines}
Summary statistics with logistic regression are the baseline for the
feature methods, and the GRU and LSTM are the baselines for the
continuous-time networks.

\subsubsection*{Metrics}
We report test accuracy and macro-F1 (the F1 score averaged over the
classes, so that every class counts equally), the training time (feature
extraction and fitting, or the training loop), and the model size: the
number of features or of trainable parameters. Clean data ($p = 0$)
answers RQ1, the comparison across $p$ answers RQ2, and the comparison of
accuracy against time and size answers RQ3.
